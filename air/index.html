Today

A five-star terminal next to a 1970s one

Walk out of Terminal A and you are in the airport the plan wants: glass, daylight, local food, public art, a Skytrax five-star rating. Walk to Terminal B and you are back in 1973.

The airport is one of the region's economic engines. The three Port Authority airports support 330,440 jobs and $75.8 billion a year in sales, and set passenger records in each of the last two years. The gap between the terminals is the gap the plan closes.

1973the year the original Terminal A opened; its replacement took until 2023
Why now

Finishing what was started

The last plan paid for Terminal A, wrote the EWR Vision Plan to carry its standard across the airport, and broke ground on the AirTrain. The agency's own phrase for this decade is “finish what was started.”

It is doing it without tax money. The Port Authority is self-supporting; airports are funded by landing fees, leases, passenger facility charges and private partners. Two-thirds of the agency's revenue is neither tolls nor fares.

By 2035

One airport, one standard

A new Terminal B, an expanded Terminal A, an updated Terminal C, a third major taxiway to cut delays, a simplified roadway, and an AirTrain that reaches the rail station. The reel at the top of this site is drawn from the agency's renderings of exactly these.

Across the three airports the agency calls it a $50 billion remaking, “from worst to first.” Newark is the last of the three to get there.
