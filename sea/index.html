Deeper water, bigger ships.

The largest port on the East Coast handles about 14 percent of the country's international cargo. The plan deepens the harbor to 55 feet, rebuilds sixty-year-old wharves, and keeps it the port ships call first.

Today

The port you drive past at seventy

From the Turnpike the cranes rise out of the marsh at Exit 13A. Behind them: six terminals, four on-dock rail yards, five marine terminals, and a workforce of more than 330,000 jobs and nearly $21 billion in wages. It reaches 36 million consumers within a day's drive.

It is also the place the modern container was invented. The Ideal X sailed from here in 1956, and in 1962 the agency opened the world's first container port at Elizabeth.

Why now

The ships got bigger. The port kept up.

The last plan raised the Bayonne Bridge to 215 feet, a world first, and deepened the harbor to 50 feet so the largest ships could call. It finished ExpressRail Bayonne, the last of four on-dock rail terminals, and started the $220 million rebuild of the Port Street Corridor. Through the pandemic the port avoided the congestion seen elsewhere and became the second-busiest in the country.

The next generation of ships needs five more feet of water.

By 2035

Fifty-five feet, sixty wharves

Deepen the channels to 55 feet with the Army Corps of Engineers. Restore or replace more than 60 wharves and berths, many past sixty years old. Open the rebuilt Port Street Corridor. And draw $1.2 billion of private investment into the terminals themselves, to keep the port the one carriers must call.

The agency's case is plain: regional jobs, trade, and the national economy that depends on it.
