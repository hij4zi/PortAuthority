    The departure board as table of contents

    The page opens on a split-flap board that clatters into place with one real morning's schedule. Each row is a scene: click it and the page jumps there. The board is the navigation, the way the clip windows are on the landing page.

    Written piece: "The first flight out", on the people already at work when the first passenger arrives.

    In the plan ($20.7B, nearly half of it): a new Terminal B, an expanded Terminal A, the $3.5B AirTrain opening 2030, the NEC station link opening 2026, a third taxiway. The last row of the board belongs to tomorrow: it flips to TERMINAL B.

    Six crossings, drawn to scale

    The Port Authority's first job was crossings. Here all six sit on one baseline at the same scale, bridges above it and tunnels below, so the reader sees that the George Washington Bridge is a different order of thing from the Outerbridge. Hover or tap for the facts.

    Written piece: "Wrong way, on purpose", on the 2.5-mile contraflow bus lane that carries over 1,850 buses every weekday morning and saves each rider 15 to 20 minutes.

    In the plan ($15.4B, the largest share after airports): the $11B Midtown Bus Terminal, phase 1 in 2030 and the main hall with a 3.5-acre park in 2035; the $2B "Restore the George"; rehabilitating the Helix. The Land page ends where the bus lane does, at the terminal being replaced.

    The dot that freezes

    Your footage already shows it: everyone checks the map under the river, and the blue dot stalls at Exchange Place until the train surfaces at the World Trade Center. On the page, a small map follows the reader's scroll. When the train enters the tunnel, the dot stops and the phone loses signal. It jumps the moment the train arrives.

    Written piece: "Four minutes under the Hudson", on the one stretch of the commute with no signal. Newark to World Trade Center is 22 minutes at any hour.

    In the plan ($2.6B): "every line, every day". All four lines seven days a week in 2026, direct weekend service, more rush-hour trains, new uptown tracks, new fare gates. The map on this page can light up the lines that run on weekends for the first time in 25 years.

    Follow one box

    The port has no timetable and no fare, so the page follows a single container instead. The reader scans its number and the page traces the box from the ship's deck to a shelf in the region, scene by scene. Each stage is a clip; the trace is the scroll.

    Written piece: "What's in the box", on what the region actually imports through Newark. The port moved 8.9 million TEUs in 2025 and handles about 14% of all US international cargo.

    In the plan ($2B plus $1.2B private): deepen the harbor from 50 to 55 feet, rebuild 60-year-old wharves, finish the Port Street Corridor. The box trace gains a last stop: the channel depth, 50 ft today, 55 ft by the end of the plan.

      What ties it together

      Two spines across four pages: the hour of the day for how the network works now, and the year in the plan for what it becomes. One signature interaction and one reported piece per page, and a fifth page on how it was made. The landing page stays as it is: the four windows are the front door.

      The agency's own framing is "finish what was started". Every mode page can close on the same beat: here is the facility in use, here is the year it changes, here is the number. That is the story the Port Authority already tells about itself, told from the platform instead of the boardroom.
